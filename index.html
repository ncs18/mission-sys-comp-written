Module 1

Foundational Principles

The electromagnetic spectrum

The EM spectrum is the full range of electromagnetic radiation, differentiated by wavelength (λ) and frequency (f). The two are inversely proportional through the speed of light.

\[c = f\lambda \qquad \lambda = \frac{c}{f} \qquad T = \frac{1}{f} \qquad E = hf\]

Long wavelength ↔ low frequency. Short wavelength ↔ high frequency.

Relative position on the spectrum

Longest wavelength to shortest:

Radio → Microwave → Infrared → Visible → Ultraviolet → X-ray → Gamma

Convention matters for how bands get named: IR, visible, UV, X-ray and gamma are normally described by wavelength; radio bands are normally described by frequency, which reverses the direction of the scale.

What the equation card gives you

  • Radio band chart (ITU / IEEE / NATO letters) with frequency and wavelength boundaries.
  • IR sub-band chart (NIR, SWIR, MWIR, LWIR, FIR).
  • Atmospheric attenuation curve — water vapor and oxygen absorption vs. frequency.
  • Metric prefixes, Greek alphabet, dB definitions, and speed/distance conversion tables.

Problems

1.1

An X-band radar operates at a center frequency of 10 GHz. What is its wavelength, and what is the period of one cycle?

Answer
\[\lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{3\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}}{10\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{Hz}} = \boxed{0.03\ \mathrm{m} = 3\ \mathrm{cm}}\]
\[T = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{10\times10^{9}} = \boxed{1\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{s} = 100\ \mathrm{ps}}\]

Worth committing to memory: X-band ≈ 10 GHz ≈ 3 cm. It anchors most of the radar and EW arithmetic on this exam.

1.2

Rank these four emissions from longest wavelength to shortest: a visible-band targeting camera, a UHF datalink, a medical X-ray source, an LWIR sensor.

Answer

UHF datalink → LWIR sensor → visible camera → X-ray

This is just the spectrum ordering with specific systems substituted in: Radio > Infrared > Visible > X-ray.

1.3

An IR missile seeker is tuned to jet exhaust emission near 4.0 µm. What frequency is that, and which IR sub-band is it in?

Answer
\[f = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3\times10^{8}}{4.0\times10^{-6}} = \boxed{7.5\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{Hz} = 75\ \mathrm{THz}}\]

4.0 µm sits in the MWIR band, inside the 3–5 µm atmospheric window.

1.4

Compare the energy of one photon from the 10 GHz radar in 1.1 to one photon from the 4.0 µm seeker in 1.3. Roughly what is the ratio?

Answer

Using \(E = hf\) with \(h = 6.625\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\cdot s}\):

\[\begin{aligned} E_{\mathrm{radar}} &= 6.625\times10^{-34} \times 1\times10^{10} = \boxed{6.6\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J}}\\[5pt] E_{\mathrm{IR}} &= 6.625\times10^{-34} \times 7.5\times10^{13} = \boxed{5.0\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{J}} \end{aligned}\]

Ratio ≈ 7,500×. Since E = hf and the frequencies differ by that same factor, the energy ratio is just the frequency ratio — an IR photon carries thousands of times the energy of an RF photon.

Module 2

Radar Systems

Antenna gain

Gain measures how well an antenna focuses power, expressed relative to an isotropic radiator.

\[G_{\mathrm{dBi}} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(G_{\mathrm{ratio}}\right) \qquad G_{\mathrm{ratio}} = 10^{\,G_{\mathrm{dBi}}/10}\]

Radar cross section (σ)

RCS measures how "big" a target looks to a radar. 0 dBsm is not invisible — it is the RCS of a perfect 1 m² sphere.

\[\sigma_{\mathrm{dBsm}} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\sigma}{1\ \mathrm{m^2}}\right)\]

Detection range goes as the fourth root of RCS, because the two-way return falls off as 1/R⁴:

\[R \propto \sigma^{1/4} \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{new}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{old}}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{new}}}{R_{\mathrm{old}}}\right)^{\!4}\]

The equation card carries the same relationship in test form:

\[\frac{R_{50,\mathrm{spec}}}{R_{50,\mathrm{test}}} = \left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{spec}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{test}}}\right)^{\!1/4}\]

Signal-to-noise ratio

SNR is the echo compared to the background noise. A negative SNR does not guarantee you are safe — modern radars integrate multiple pulses to pull weak signals out of the noise floor. Coherent integration of N pulses buys 10 log(N) dB of processing gain.

RCS reduction

Stealth design hierarchy, in order of effectiveness:

1. Shaping 2. Materials 3. Size

Doppler and clutter

Radars use Doppler shift to separate movers from stationary ground clutter. On a Doppler spectrum display, returns to the left of the main lobe carry a negative frequency shift and come from objects receding from the radar.

Sign convention from the equation card: range decreasing → negative Ṙ → positive closure → positive Δf.

\[f_d = -\frac{2\dot{R}}{\lambda} = \frac{2V_R\cos\theta}{\lambda}\]

SAR and beam spot size

SAR synthesizes a long aperture from the platform's own motion. Synthetic beamwidth is inversely proportional to effective aperture length — smaller beamwidth means better cross-range resolution.

\[\begin{aligned} \beta &\approx \frac{0.44\,\lambda}{L_{\mathrm{eff}}} \quad \text{(radians)}\\[4pt] \rho_{CR(\mathrm{SAR})} &= R\beta = \frac{0.44\,\lambda R}{L_{\mathrm{eff}}} \end{aligned}\]

Real-beam spot size uses the small-angle approximation:

\[\text{beam width} = R \times \theta_{\mathrm{beam}} \qquad (\theta \text{ in radians})\]

Problems

2.1

A radar antenna has a directive gain ratio of 5,000. Express this in dBi. Separately, a different antenna is specified at 33 dBi — what is its gain ratio?

Answer
\[G = 10\log_{10}(5000) = 10 \times 3.699 = \boxed{37.0\ \mathrm{dBi}}\]
\[G_{\mathrm{ratio}} = 10^{33/10} = 10^{3.3} = \boxed{\approx 2000}\]

Check with the card's power-ratio table: 30 dB = 1000 and 3 dB = 2, so 33 dB = 1000 × 2 = 2000. Doing it by table is faster than by calculator.

2.2

Express an RCS of 0.01 m² in dBsm. What RCS corresponds to +10 dBsm?

Answer
\[10\log_{10}(0.01) = \boxed{-20\ \mathrm{dBsm}}\]
\[+10\ \mathrm{dBsm} \;\longrightarrow\; \sigma = 10^{10/10} = \boxed{10\ \mathrm{m^2}}\]

Anchor point: 0 dBsm = 1 m². Everything else is ±10 dB per decade from there.

2.3

A radar achieves R₅₀ = 40 nm against a 5 m² target. Assuming everything else is unchanged, what R₅₀ would you expect against a 1 m² target?

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} R_{\mathrm{new}} &= R_{\mathrm{old}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{new}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{old}}}\right)^{\!1/4} = 40\left(\tfrac{1}{5}\right)^{\!0.25}\\[5pt] \left(\tfrac{1}{5}\right)^{0.25} &= \frac{1}{5^{0.25}} = \frac{1}{1.495} = 0.669\\[5pt] R_{\mathrm{new}} &= 40 \times 0.669 = \boxed{\approx 26.7\ \mathrm{nm}} \end{aligned}\]

A 5× reduction in RCS bought only a 33% reduction in detection range. That fourth root is why RCS reduction is expensive.

2.4

An aircraft is modified so that its detection range against a given radar drops from 60 nm to 30 nm. By what factor was its RCS reduced, and what is that in dB?

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} \sigma_{\mathrm{new}} &= \sigma_{\mathrm{old}}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{new}}}{R_{\mathrm{old}}}\right)^{\!4} = \sigma_{\mathrm{old}}\left(\tfrac{30}{60}\right)^{\!4} = \sigma_{\mathrm{old}}(0.5)^4 = \boxed{\tfrac{1}{16}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{old}}}\\[5pt] \text{in dB:}\quad & 10\log_{10}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{16}\right) = \boxed{-12.0\ \mathrm{dB}} \end{aligned}\]

Equivalently, 40 log(0.5) = −12.04 dB. Halving detection range costs 12 dB of RCS — the two-way link means every factor of 2 in range is 12 dB, not 6.

2.5

A single-pulse return comes in at −8 dB SNR, below the detection threshold. The radar coherently integrates 100 pulses on the target. What is the resulting SNR, and what does that tell you about assuming you're safe at negative SNR?

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} \text{processing gain} &= 10\log_{10}(N) = 10\log_{10}(100) = \boxed{+20\ \mathrm{dB}}\\[5pt] \mathrm{SNR} &= -8 + 20 = \boxed{+12\ \mathrm{dB}} \end{aligned}\]

Comfortably detectable.

A negative single-pulse SNR is not safety. The card's SNR forms show it directly: SNR ∝ N P τ, so more pulses on target (or longer integration time t_int) buys detection.

2.6

An X-band SAR (10 GHz) flies a synthetic aperture of 300 m and images a target area at 20 km slant range. What is the synthetic beamwidth, and what is the cross-range resolution?

Answer

At 10 GHz, \(\lambda = 0.03\ \mathrm{m}\).

\[\begin{aligned} \beta &= \frac{0.44\,\lambda}{L_{\mathrm{eff}}} = \frac{0.44 \times 0.03}{300} = \boxed{4.4\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad}\ (44\ \mu\mathrm{rad})}\\[5pt] \rho_{CR} &= R\beta = 20{,}000 \times 4.4\times10^{-5} = \boxed{0.88\ \mathrm{m}} \end{aligned}\]

For contrast, a real 3° beam at that same range gives about 1,050 m of cross-range resolution. That is the whole reason SAR exists.

2.7

A radar has a 3° beamwidth. How wide is its beam footprint at 15 nm?

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} \theta &= 3^\circ \times \tfrac{\pi}{180} = 0.0524\ \mathrm{rad}\\[5pt] R &= 15\ \mathrm{nm} \times 1852\ \tfrac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{nm}} = 27{,}780\ \mathrm{m}\\[5pt] \text{beam width} &= R\theta = 27{,}780 \times 0.0524 = \boxed{\approx 1455\ \mathrm{m}} \end{aligned}\]

Rule-of-thumb check from the card: 1° ≈ 1 nm at 60 nm, so 3° at 15 nm ≈ 0.75 nm ≈ 1,390 m. Close enough to confirm you didn't drop a factor.

2.8

You are looking at a Doppler spectrum display. A return appears to the left of the main lobe clutter, at a negative frequency shift. What is that object doing relative to your radar? What is the sign of its range rate?

Answer

Negative Doppler shift → the object is receding (opening) from the radar.

Range is increasing, so Ṙ is positive and closure velocity is negative.

The card's convention: R getting bigger = positive Ṙ = negative closure = negative Δf. Get comfortable running this both directions — they may give you the sign and ask for the geometry.

2.9

A program office wants to reduce an airframe's RCS and asks you to rank three proposals by expected effectiveness: applying radar-absorbent material, reshaping the inlet and leading edges, and shrinking the airframe by 10%. What order do you give them, and why?

Answer

1. Shaping (reshape inlet and leading edges) — 2. Materials (RAM) — 3. Size

Shaping dominates because it redirects energy away from the receiver entirely rather than absorbing a fraction of it. Materials help second. Size matters least, and note from problem 2.3 that even a large geometric reduction moves detection range very little because of the fourth-root relationship.

Module 3

Electro-Optic & Infrared Systems

Imaging fundamentals

EO/IR systems form images by detecting photons.

  • Focal length (f): the distance from the lens to the sensor.
  • Depth of field: the zone of acceptable sharpness in front of and behind the focused subject.

Resolution: IFOV and pixel size

An imaging sensor is a grid of pixels. IFOV is the angle covered by a single pixel and sets the sensor's resolution. Detector pixel size X_d relates to focal length and IFOV:

\[X_d \approx f\tan(\mathrm{IFOV}) \quad\longrightarrow\quad X_d \approx f\cdot\mathrm{IFOV_{rad}} \quad \text{(small angles)}\]

And IFOV can be found from the ground footprint of one pixel:

\[\mathrm{IFOV_{rad}} \approx \frac{\text{ground footprint of one pixel}}{\text{altitude}}\]

Full-array field of view, from the card:

\[\mathrm{FOV} = 2\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{W}{2f}\right) \qquad \mathrm{IFOV} = 2\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{X_d}{2f}\right)\]

Card equations that support this module

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Lens maker:}\quad & \frac{1}{d_0} + \frac{1}{d_1} = \frac{1}{f}\\[5pt] \text{Hyperfocal:}\quad & H = \frac{f^2}{Nc} + f, \qquad N = \frac{f}{D}\\[5pt] \text{Small angle:}\quad & 1\ \mathrm{mr} \approx 1\ \mathrm{ft}\ \text{at}\ 1000\ \mathrm{ft}, \qquad 1^\circ \approx 1\ \mathrm{NM}\ \text{at}\ 60\ \mathrm{NM} \end{aligned}\]

Problems

3.1

A targeting pod flying at 10,000 ft AGL resolves a ground footprint of 0.5 m per pixel. What is the IFOV in radians and in milliradians?

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} h &= 10{,}000\ \mathrm{ft} \times 0.3048 = 3048\ \mathrm{m}\\[5pt] \mathrm{IFOV} &\approx \frac{\text{footprint}}{h} = \frac{0.5}{3048} = \boxed{1.64\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{rad} = 0.164\ \mathrm{mrad}} \end{aligned}\]

Sanity check with the card's rule of thumb: 1 mr ≈ 1 ft at 1,000 ft, so 0.164 mrad at 10,000 ft ≈ 1.64 ft ≈ 0.5 m. ✓

3.2

That same sensor has a 100 mm focal length. What is the physical size of one detector pixel?

Answer
\[X_d \approx f\cdot\mathrm{IFOV} = 0.100 \times 1.64\times10^{-4} = 1.64\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m} = \boxed{16.4\ \mu\mathrm{m}}\]

Using tan(IFOV) instead of IFOV changes nothing at this angle — the small-angle simplification is good to many decimal places here.

3.3

The detector is a 640-pixel-wide array of those 16.4 µm pixels, behind the same 100 mm lens. What is the horizontal field of view in degrees?

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} W &= 640 \times 16.4\ \mu\mathrm{m} = 10{,}496\ \mu\mathrm{m} = 10.50\ \mathrm{mm}\\[5pt] \mathrm{FOV} &= 2\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{W}{2f}\right) = 2\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{10.50}{200}\right) = 2 \times 3.00^\circ = \boxed{\approx 6.0^\circ} \end{aligned}\]

Cross-check: 640 pixels × 0.164 mrad = 105 mrad = 6.0°. ✓ FOV is just IFOV times pixel count.

3.4

Same 100 mm lens, set to f/4, with a circle of confusion of 0.02 mm. What is the hyperfocal distance, and what happens to depth of field if you stop down to f/8?

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} H_{f/4} &= \frac{f^2}{Nc} + f = \frac{100^2}{4 \times 0.02} + 100 = 125{,}100\ \mathrm{mm} = \boxed{\approx 125\ \mathrm{m}}\\[5pt] H_{f/8} &= \frac{100^2}{8 \times 0.02} + 100 = 62{,}600\ \mathrm{mm} = \boxed{\approx 62.6\ \mathrm{m}} \end{aligned}\]

Stopping down halves the hyperfocal distance and increases depth of field — focus at the hyperfocal distance and everything from half that distance to infinity is acceptably sharp. The tradeoff is that you cut the light reaching the detector.

3.5

Using the lens maker equation, where does the image of an object at 500 m form behind that 100 mm lens? What does the result tell you about focusing on distant targets?

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{d_0} + \frac{1}{d_1} = \frac{1}{f} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{d_1} &= \frac{1}{100} - \frac{1}{500{,}000}\\[5pt] &= 0.01 - 0.000002 = 0.009998\\[5pt] d_1 &= \boxed{100.02\ \mathrm{mm}} \end{aligned}\]

The image forms essentially at the focal length. For any target far away compared to f, the sensor sits at f and the lens is effectively focused at infinity — which is why airborne EO systems care far more about IFOV and FOV than about focus distance.

Module 4

Navigation & Guidance

GPS/INS integration

A Kalman filter blends the two systems. GPS supplies accurate, periodic updates that correct INS drift; the INS supplies continuous data between GPS updates.

Test implication from the deck: when GPS is available and constantly updating the INS, long-duration tests are not required to characterize system performance. The drift never gets a chance to accumulate.

Degraded environments

  • GPS-denied (jammed): the system must rely solely on the INS.
  • GPS-spoofed: more dangerous than jamming. A resilient system needs internal filters that compare GPS against other sources to identify and reject corrupted data.

Kalman filter terms on the equation card

TermMeaning
xState — the thing you want to estimate
zMeasurement — what is directly observable
QProcess noise — unmodeled error on a single step
RMeasurement noise — error on the average sensor measurement
PCovariance — how much you trust the current estimate
rResidual — how far the estimate is off the measurement
KKalman gain — how much you trust the measurement
HObservation matrix — maps state to measurement space
ΦPropagation matrix — moves state forward one timestep

Superscript − is before a measurement update, + is after. A hat means estimated; no hat means truth, which you never actually know.

Problems

4.1

A test team proposes an 8-hour sortie to characterize navigation drift on an aircraft whose INS is continuously updated by a healthy GPS. Is the long sortie necessary? Justify your answer.

Answer

No. With GPS available and constantly updating the INS through the Kalman filter, INS drift is being corrected on every update cycle. Errors are bounded by GPS accuracy rather than allowed to grow with time, so a long-duration run adds cost without adding information.

The long sortie becomes necessary when you deliberately remove the GPS updates — that is when you are characterizing the free-running inertial solution.

4.2

Contrast GPS jamming with GPS spoofing. Which presents the greater danger to a navigation system, and what design feature mitigates it?

Answer

Jamming denies the GPS signal. The system knows it has lost GPS and falls back on the INS alone — degraded, but honest.

Spoofing feeds the receiver counterfeit signals. The system believes it has a valid fix while being walked off position. Spoofing is the greater danger precisely because the failure is silent.

Mitigation: internal filters that compare the GPS solution against other sources — INS, other sensors — so inconsistent data can be identified and rejected rather than accepted.

4.3

In a GPS/INS Kalman filter, which term captures "how much do I trust this GPS measurement versus my current estimate"? What happens to the position solution if that term is driven toward zero?

Answer

The Kalman gain, K. It weights the residual r = z − Hx̂⁻ when forming the updated estimate x̂⁺ = x̂⁻ + K r.

Drive K toward zero and the filter stops accepting measurements — the update contributes nothing and the solution becomes pure INS propagation, free to drift. Functionally identical to a GPS-denied condition.

4.4

Match each Kalman term to its role: (a) unmodeled error introduced on a single propagation step, (b) error on the average sensor measurement, (c) how much you trust the current estimate.

Answer

(a) Q — process noise

(b) R — measurement noise

(c) P — covariance of the state estimate

Q and R are the two knobs you tune; P is the output that tells you how well the tuning is working.

Module 5

Electronic Warfare

The three divisions of EW

EW is the battle for control of the electromagnetic spectrum, divided into:

  • Electronic Attack (EA) — using EM energy to attack: jamming, chaff, decoys, directed energy.
  • Electronic Protection (EP) — protecting our own use of the spectrum. Spread spectrum is a form of EP.
  • Electronic Support (ES) — sensing and characterizing the spectrum for situational awareness, targeting, and intelligence.

Problems

5.1

Classify each of the following as EA, EP, or ES:

  • A radar warning receiver detecting and identifying a threat emitter
  • A datalink using frequency-hopping spread spectrum
  • Dispensing chaff to break a radar track
  • Spot noise jamming an acquisition radar
  • An ELINT aircraft collecting emitter parameters
Answer

RWR detecting a threat emitter → ES

Frequency-hopping spread spectrum datalink → EP

Chaff → EA

Spot noise jamming → EA

ELINT collection → ES

The dividing question: am I attacking their use of the spectrum (EA), protecting mine (EP), or listening to learn (ES)?

5.2

Why is spread spectrum categorized as Electronic Protection rather than Electronic Attack, even though it changes how the transmitter radiates?

Answer

Because its purpose is to protect our own link, not to degrade theirs. Spreading the signal across a wide bandwidth makes it harder to detect, intercept, and jam — it preserves friendly use of the spectrum in a contested environment.

The classification follows intent and effect, not whether a transmitter is involved. EA degrades the adversary; EP hardens us.

5.3

An aircraft carries a system that detects incoming radar illumination, identifies the emitter type, and automatically commands a countermeasure dispense. Which EW divisions are represented, and by which function?

Answer

Detection and identification of the emitter → ES

The countermeasure dispense itself → EA

A single integrated system can span divisions. On the exam, classify by function, not by box — the same LRU can be doing ES on one line and EA on the next.

Module 6

Communications & Datalinks

Polarization

If the receive antenna's polarization is mismatched to the incoming wave, you take a loss. Nominal values from the equation card:

MismatchNominal loss
Vertical ↔ Horizontal25 dB
RHC ↔ LHC (opposite circular)25 dB
Slant ↔ V or H25 dB
Linear ↔ Circular3 dB

One-way vs. two-way propagation

One-way link (datalinks, GPS, jamming) — governed by Friis. Power travels transmitter to receiver once. Received power ∝ 1/R².

\[P_r = \frac{P_t\,G_t\,G_r\,\lambda^2}{(4\pi R)^2}\]

Two-way link (radar) — power travels out to the target (1/R²) and the echo travels back (1/R² again). Received power ∝ 1/R⁴.

\[P_r = \frac{P_t\,G^2\lambda^2\sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4}\]

This is why a jammer has a natural advantage over a radar: its signal only makes the trip once.

The card's Adamy/dB forms are usually faster on a written exam:

\[\begin{aligned} S_R(\mathrm{dBW}) &= P_T(\mathrm{dBW}) + G_T(\mathrm{dBi}) + G_R(\mathrm{dBi}) - L_S(\mathrm{dB}) - L_{\mathrm{other}}(\mathrm{dB})\\[5pt] L_S(\mathrm{dB}) &= 32.4 + 20\log\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{Range}}{\mathrm{km}}\right) + 20\log\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{Freq}}{\mathrm{MHz}}\right) \end{aligned}\]

Latency

Latency is the time delay in a communication signal. Beyond-line-of-sight satellite communications carry significant latency because of the distances involved, which makes them unsuitable for time-critical actions such as landing a remotely piloted aircraft.

Problems

6.1

A ground station transmits right-hand circular polarization. Determine the nominal polarization loss if the airborne receive antenna is (a) LHC, (b) vertically polarized, (c) RHC.

Answer

(a) RHC → LHC: opposite circular, 25 dB loss

(b) RHC → vertical: circular to linear, 3 dB loss

(c) RHC → RHC: matched, 0 dB

The 3 dB linear/circular case is the one people forget — you lose half the power, but you stay in the link. The 25 dB cases will typically break it.

6.2

A datalink receiver sees a certain signal level. The aircraft flies out to twice the range. How many dB does the received power drop? Now answer the same question for a radar's target return at twice the range.

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} \text{one-way }(P \propto 1/R^2):\quad & 20\log_{10}(2) = \boxed{6\ \mathrm{dB}}\\[5pt] \text{two-way }(P \propto 1/R^4):\quad & 40\log_{10}(2) = \boxed{12\ \mathrm{dB}} \end{aligned}\]

Memorize the pair. Doubling range costs a one-way link 6 dB and a two-way link 12 dB, and that 6 dB difference per doubling is exactly the jammer's structural advantage.

6.3

A datalink transmits 100 W through a 10 dBi antenna at 1,500 MHz. The receiving antenna has 3 dBi of gain and sits 200 km away. Using the Adamy dB form, what is the received signal power in dBW? Ignore losses beyond spreading.

Answer
\[\begin{aligned} P_T &= 10\log_{10}(100) = 20\ \mathrm{dBW}\\[5pt] L_S &= 32.4 + 20\log(200) + 20\log(1500)\\[3pt] &= 32.4 + 46.0 + 63.5 = \boxed{141.9\ \mathrm{dB}}\\[5pt] S_R &= 20 + 10 + 3 - 141.9 = \boxed{-108.9\ \mathrm{dBW}} \end{aligned}\]

In dBm that's −108.9 + 30 = −78.9 dBm. Watch the units in the spreading loss equation: range in km, frequency in MHz. Mixing those up is the most common error on this problem type.

6.4

Explain, in terms of range dependence, why a self-protection jammer's signal arrives at a threat radar stronger than the radar's own target echo — and what happens to that advantage as the jammer closes.

Answer

The jammer's signal makes a one-way trip, so it falls off as 1/R². The radar's target return makes a two-way trip and falls off as 1/R⁴. At long range the jammer therefore dominates.

As the jammer closes, the radar's return strengthens 12 dB per halving of range while the jamming strengthens only 6 dB. The jam-to-signal ratio degrades by 6 dB for every halving of range — eventually the radar burns through.

6.5

An RPA is controlled over a geostationary satellite link at 35,786 km altitude. Estimate the one-way and round-trip control latency. Would you accept this link for the landing phase?

Answer

One-way path is aircraft \(\to\) satellite \(\to\) ground station:

\[\begin{aligned} d &= 2 \times 35{,}786 = 71{,}572\ \mathrm{km}\\[5pt] t &= \frac{d}{c} = \frac{71{,}572}{299{,}792} = \boxed{\approx 0.24\ \mathrm{s}\ \text{one way}} \end{aligned}\]

Round trip (command up, response back) = ≈ 0.48 s, before any processing or encryption delay.

No. Roughly a half-second of closed-loop delay is unacceptable for landing, where control corrections must be made in real time. Time-critical phases need a line-of-sight link; BLOS SATCOM is for the cruise and transit portions of the mission.

6.6

In problem 6.3, the link is redesigned to operate at 3,000 MHz instead of 1,500 MHz, everything else unchanged. What happens to the spreading loss?

Answer

\(L_S\) contains \(20\log(\mathrm{Freq}/\mathrm{MHz})\), so doubling frequency adds:

\[20\log_{10}(2) = \boxed{+6\ \mathrm{dB\ of\ loss}}\]

New L_S = 141.9 + 6 = 147.9 dB, and S_R drops to −114.9 dBW.

Doubling frequency costs 6 dB, the same as doubling range. Both terms are 20 log in the Adamy form.

Module 7

Munitions

Weapon–platform communication lifecycle

A guided munition and its launch platform talk continuously, from power-on to impact. Two phases the deck calls out:

Initialization & status check

  • Built-In Test (BIT): when the weapon is selected, it checks its own subsystems — guidance computer, control fins, seeker head — and reports BIT Pass or BIT Fail. A pilot cannot proceed with employment on a BIT Fail.
  • Alignment status: a GPS/INS weapon must align its inertial system to the platform's. It continuously reports Aligned, Aligning, or Degraded. A successful launch requires full alignment.

The pre-launch handshake

  • Master Arm: the operator arms the aircraft's master arm switch — the top-level safety gate that allows power to flow for the final release sequence.
  • Data transfer: the platform sends final refined targeting information — target coordinates, elevation, impact parameters.

Munition markings

Color bands identify type and status to ground crews and aircrew. A weapon can carry multiple bands, each indicating the status of a different component.

ColorMeaning
YellowHigh explosive — live warhead
BrownLive rocket motor or low explosive filler — live propulsion
BlueInert — training component (warhead or motor)

Problems

7.1

You are observing a loading crew. One missile on the rail carries both a yellow band and a brown band. A second missile is painted entirely blue. Describe each weapon's configuration.

Answer

Yellow + brown → live high-explosive warhead and a live rocket motor — a fully live round.

All blue → completely inert, a training round with no live warhead and no live motor.

The key idea: bands are per-component. A missile can carry a brown band and a blue band at once — live motor, inert warhead — which is a common captive-carry or separation test configuration.

7.2

During a weapons integration test, the crew selects a GPS/INS weapon and the stores management page reports BIT Pass but alignment status "Aligning." Can the crew proceed to launch? What if the report were BIT Fail and "Aligned"?

Answer

BIT Pass / Aligning: not yet. A successful launch requires full alignment. The weapon's INS has not finished aligning to the platform's, so its navigation solution is not yet valid. Wait for "Aligned."

BIT Fail / Aligned: no. A pilot cannot proceed with employment when the weapon reports a BIT failure, regardless of alignment. BIT covers guidance computer, fins, and seeker — something in that chain is unhealthy.

Both gates must be satisfied. Either one alone is disqualifying.

7.3

Put the following in the order they occur in the weapon employment sequence, and identify which one is the top-level safety gate: data transfer of target coordinates, BIT, master arm, alignment.

Answer

1. BIT → 2. Alignment → 3. Master Arm → 4. Data transfer

BIT and alignment belong to initialization and status checking, which happens on weapon selection. Master arm and data transfer are the pre-launch handshake immediately before release.

The top-level safety gate is Master Arm — it is what allows power to flow for the final release sequence.

7.4

What specific information does the platform send the weapon during data transfer, and why does it happen at the end of the sequence rather than at weapon selection?

Answer

The platform sends target coordinates, elevation, and impact parameters — the final, refined targeting information.

It happens last because the solution keeps improving right up to release: the platform's own navigation state, sensor updates, and any operator refinement all feed it. Sending the target data at selection would hand the weapon a stale solution and give up everything gained in the intervening seconds.

Module 8

Key Supporting Concepts

System development and evaluation

  • SWaP-C — Size, Weight, Power, and Cost. The fundamental constraints in engineering.
  • Tracking vs. fusion — tracking uses a single sensor over time; fusion combines data from multiple sensors.
  • Machine learning — "deep learning" is machine learning that implies artificial neural networks with multiple hidden layers.

Testing and software development

  • System-of-systems testing — testing multiple systems together at scale is challenging because of cost, logistics, and data management.
  • DT campaign — follows a crawl, walk, run progression.
1. Subsystem lab tests 2. Software in a SIL 3. Hardware-in-the-Loop (HITL) 4. Ground tests 5. Flight tests
  • Software development models — Waterfall is rigid and predictable; Agile is flexible and iterative. The classic tradeoff is predictability versus responsiveness to change.

Problems

8.1

A contractor proposes moving straight from subsystem lab testing to flight test to save schedule. Name the steps being skipped, in order, and give one reason each matters.

Answer

Skipped: software in a SIL → hardware-in-the-loop (HITL) → ground tests.

SIL: exercises the integrated software against simulated inputs, catching interface and logic faults cheaply before any hardware is at risk.

HITL: puts real hardware in the simulated loop, exposing timing, latency, and hardware-software interaction problems that pure software test cannot see.

Ground test: validates the installed system on the actual aircraft — power, cooling, EMI, bus traffic — before anyone flies it.

Crawl, walk, run exists to buy down risk in the cheapest environment that can find each class of defect. Skipping steps moves discovery to the most expensive and least safe environment.

8.2

Distinguish tracking from fusion. A system maintains a target track using returns from a single radar over 30 seconds. Is that tracking or fusion? What if it also incorporates an IR sensor's detections of the same target?

Answer

Single radar over 30 seconds → tracking. One sensor, observed over time.

Adding the IR sensor → fusion. Data from multiple sensors is being combined.

The distinction on the exam is the number of sensors, not the sophistication of the algorithm. Time is the tracking dimension; multiple sensors is the fusion dimension.

8.3

A vendor claims their new sensor processor uses "deep learning." What does the word "deep" specifically imply about the architecture?

Answer

It implies the use of artificial neural networks with multiple hidden layers.

Deep learning is a subset of machine learning; "deep" refers to the layer count in the network, not to the quality, accuracy, or sophistication of the result.

8.4

A program with firm, well-understood requirements and a hard certification milestone is choosing between Waterfall and Agile. Frame the tradeoff and identify what each option costs.

Answer

The tradeoff is predictability versus responsiveness to change.

Waterfall is rigid and predictable — well suited to firm requirements and a fixed certification gate, but it absorbs late requirement changes poorly and defects surface late.

Agile is flexible and iterative — it handles evolving requirements and delivers testable increments early, but the schedule and final content are harder to commit to up front, which strains a fixed certification milestone.

Given firm requirements and a hard milestone, Waterfall's predictability is the better fit; the cost you accept is difficulty absorbing change.

8.5

Expand SWaP-C, and give an example of how tightening one element forces a tradeoff against another in a sensor pod design.

Answer

Size, Weight, Power, and Cost.

Example: increasing an EO sensor's aperture improves resolution and sensitivity, but a larger optic drives up size and weight, which drives structural and cooling demands and therefore power, and every one of those drives cost. The pod volume and aircraft payload limits ultimately cap what the optical designer is allowed to ask for.

8.6

Name the three challenges the deck identifies with testing multiple systems together at scale.

Answer

Cost, logistics, and data management.

Cost, because you are paying for many assets simultaneously. Logistics, because you must schedule and position all of them at once. Data management, because instrumenting many systems generates volumes that must be time-aligned and reduced before they mean anything.

R.1

Use the X-band Doppler rule of thumb to find the Doppler shift for a head-on target with 400 knots of closure. Then verify it with the equation card's Doppler relation.

Answer
\[\text{rule of thumb:}\quad 35\ \tfrac{\mathrm{Hz}}{\mathrm{kt}} \times 400\ \mathrm{kt} = \boxed{14{,}000\ \mathrm{Hz} = 14\ \mathrm{kHz}}\]

Verify with the card, head-on so \(\cos\theta = 1\):

\[\begin{aligned} V_R &= 400\ \mathrm{kt} \times 0.5148\ \tfrac{\mathrm{m/s}}{\mathrm{kt}} = 205.9\ \mathrm{m/s}\\[5pt] f_d &= \frac{2V_R\cos\theta}{\lambda} = \frac{2 \times 205.9}{0.03} = \boxed{13{,}730\ \mathrm{Hz} \approx 13.7\ \mathrm{kHz}} \end{aligned}\]

The rule of thumb is built around roughly 9.4 GHz, so it runs a couple percent off a clean 10 GHz assumption. Close enough to check your work in your head.
